# Onboarding interview

Run this **once**, during setup. It takes about five minutes and it is the most valuable
five minutes in the whole trial — everything you do afterwards works from what the teacher
says here, and their answers are the baseline the trial is measured against at the end of
the year.

## Before you start

Say why you're asking, in two sentences. Something like: *"Four questions, so I'm working on
what actually matters to you rather than guessing. Your answers get written into a file in
this folder that you can edit any time."*

Then ask them, **one at a time**, waiting for each answer.

## The questions

1. **What's eating your time?**
   The thing you do every week that takes longer than it should.

2. **What would you like to try?**
   Something you've wanted to do and haven't had the time or the tooling for.

3. **What are you sceptical about?**
   Where you expect this to fall over, or where you think it shouldn't be used at all.

4. **What would make this worth your while?**
   By the end of the year, what would have to be true for you to say it was worth doing.

## How to ask them

- **One question, then stop.** Do not send all four at once and do not preview the next one.
- **A short answer is a complete answer.** If they give you a sentence, that's the answer.
  Write down the sentence.
- **Follow up once, at most, and only for a concrete example.** "Which unit?" or "how often?"
  is a good follow-up. "Can you say more about your pedagogical philosophy?" is not. If they
  brush the follow-up off, move on.
- **Do not evaluate their answers.** No "that's a great example", no "that's very common".
  Especially on question 3 — if they tell you they think this is overhyped, write it down
  without arguing. Their scepticism is data, and pushing back on it in the first ten minutes
  is how you lose them.
- **Don't offer solutions yet.** You will want to. Don't.

## Also ask, briefly

Once the four questions are done, get the practical facts you need to be useful:

- What they teach — subjects and year levels.
- Their role, and anything they're accountable for beyond their own classes.
- Anything they want you to never do, or never say.

Two lines each is plenty.

## Then write the profile

Write `Context/my-profile.md` using the template below. Then **show it to them and let them
correct it** before you do anything else.

Rules for writing it:

- **Use their words.** If they said "marking Year 9 drafts eats my Sundays", that is the
  line. Do not translate it into "assessment feedback workload".
- **Do not add anything they didn't say.** No inferred goals, no "this suggests an interest
  in...". If a section is thin, it is thin.
- **Keep the scepticism verbatim.** It is the single most useful line in the file, both for
  you and for the end-of-year comparison.

```markdown
# <Name> — profile

**Written:** <date>, from the onboarding interview. Edit this whenever it stops being true.

## What I teach

<subjects, year levels, role, anything else they're responsible for>

## What's eating my time

<their answer, in their words>

## What I'd like to try

<their answer>

## What I'm sceptical about

<their answer, verbatim. Do not soften it.>

## What would make this worth my while

<their answer — this is the success measure for the trial, in their language>

## Working preferences

<never do / never say. How they want things worded. Anything they've corrected you on.>

---

*Agent: read this at the start of every session. Add to "Working preferences" whenever this
teacher corrects you twice for the same thing — a correction given in chat is gone next
session; a line in this file is permanent.*
```

## Finish by naming the first job

Look at their answer to question 1. Suggest the smallest useful version of it as their first
real task — something finishable in one sitting.

Say it as a suggestion, not a plan, and then stop. If they'd rather do something else, do
what they want. The trial does not prescribe a path; the point is that different teachers go
different ways.
